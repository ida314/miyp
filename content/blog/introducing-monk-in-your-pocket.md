Most AI chatbots that touch on Buddhist practice share the same flaw: they sound like Buddhism without being grounded in it. Ask one about anger and you'll get a confident, vaguely Zen-inflected paragraph that might have come from anywhere. Ask for a source and you'll get silence, a hallucinated citation, or a link to a Wikipedia article.

We wanted to do something different.

## The problem with Buddhist AI

The issue isn't that LLMs don't know enough Buddhism. They've ingested enormous amounts of it. The problem is that they have no reliable mechanism for *retrieving* specific teachings in response to specific problems — and no way to cite what they're drawing on.

The standard solution in AI is retrieval-augmented generation: retrieve relevant passages from a corpus, then generate a response grounded in those passages. But naive RAG over raw sutta text has its own problem. The Pāli Canon was not written for keyword search. Its passages are often dialogic, heavily repetitive, written for a monastic audience, and phrased in ways that don't map naturally to how a modern person would describe their problem.

If someone types "I keep picking fights with my partner and I don't know why," the relevant passage might be AN 5.162 — the five ways of removing annoyance. But that passage begins "There are these five ways of removing annoyance, bhikkhus." Neither the word "partner" nor "fight" appears. Naive semantic search will frequently miss it.

## The structured advice unit

Our solution is a layer of structured extraction between the raw canon and the retrieval index. We call the extracted units *AdviceUnits*.

Each AdviceUnit is generated by an LLM that reads a sutta passage and answers: what human problem does this address, in modern language? What is the Buddhist diagnostic interpretation? What is the key teaching? What can a lay practitioner actually *do*?

The result is a structured record that captures the practical essence of the passage in vocabulary that maps to how real people describe their problems — without losing the source text or the citation.

When a user asks about picking fights with their partner, the retrieval system matches their description against these normalized problem summaries and diagnostic labels, not against the raw sutta text. Then the final response is built from the retrieved units *and* cites back to the original passage.

## What we're building toward

The initial corpus covers selected vaggas from the Aṅguttara Nikāya and Majjhima Nikāya, with Saṁyutta Nikāya planned. We're focusing on eight problem categories that represent the most common lay concerns: anger and aversion, craving and attachment, anxiety and fear, grief and loss, restlessness and distraction, ethical conflict, doubt and indecision, and difficulties in speech and relationships.

This is a deliberately scoped MVP. The goal is to do a small number of things well, with verifiable grounding, before expanding coverage.
